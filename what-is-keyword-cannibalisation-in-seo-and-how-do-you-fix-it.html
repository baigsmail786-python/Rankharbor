What Is Keyword Cannibalisation in SEO and How Do You Fix It?

Keyword cannibalisation in SEO happens when two or more pages on your site chase the same search phrase. Learn how to spot the fight in Search Console and fix it with merges, retargeting, and internal links.

Short answer: Keyword cannibalisation in SEO happens when two or more pages on your site chase the same search phrase. Google cannot tell which page deserves the rank, so the pages split clicks and links.

Most site owners write a new post for every variation of a phrase. They end up with thin pages that fight each other for one search. They blame Google when the pages swap places, and the cause sits on their site.

You will learn how to spot the pages, pick the winner, and fix the rest with a merge or a new target. Your site will send one strong page to each search.

What Keyword Cannibalisation Means

When readers ask what is keyword cannibalisation in SEO, they want a plain answer. Your own pages compete with each other for one search, and Google must choose one of them for the results. Google often picks neither, and both pages land lower than one page would. The phrase still brings searches, and your site still misses the top spots.

A small plumbing site wrote one guide on fixing a leaking tap and one service page on tap repair, and both pages chased the phrase fix a leaking tap. Google showed the guide one week and the service page the next, and neither page held a steady spot. The site merged the two pages into one strong guide with a clear booking link, and the single page soon outranked both old pages.

Cannibalisation differs from healthy overlap. Two pages can cover related topics without harm when each one answers a different question. The trouble starts when both pages chase the same phrase with the same answer.

How You Spot Cannibalisation on Your Own Site

You spot the problem by watching which pages Google shows for one phrase. Search Console shows the queries that bring each page into the results, and the same query next to two pages flags the fight. You open the performance report, filter it by one query, and read the list of pages that appear for it. Two pages on one query means you found a fight.

Check Search Console for the Same Query

You open Search Console, pick your site, and open the performance report. You click one query that matters to your business, and you read the pages tab below it. The tab lists every page that appeared for that query, and two or more pages on the list means those pages share one target.

Compare the Competing Pages Side by Side

You open the two pages side by side and read what each one promises. You check the title, the first paragraph, and the main headings of each page. Pages that promise the same answer to the same question compete, and pages that promise different answers serve different readers. A bakery guide on sourdough starters and a bakery product page for sourdough bread serve different readers, but two guides on sourdough starters fight and one of them must change.

Why Cannibalisation Hurts Your Rankings

People who ask what is keyword cannibalisation in SEO often want to know why it hurts rankings. The harm comes from split signals, and split signals weaken every page in the fight. Links point at two pages instead of one, and each page earns half the strength that one page would earn. Google also reads two similar pages as a sign that your site lacks one clear answer.

A travel blog wrote three posts on cheap hotels in Bangkok, and each post earned only a few links. The blog merged the three posts into one deep guide, and the links from all three old posts pointed at the one new page through redirects. The single guide climbed higher than any of the three old posts, and it held the spot because one page now carried all the strength.

How You Fix It Step by Step

You fix the fight by picking one winner and dealing with the rest in a clear order. The winner is the page with the most links, the most traffic, or the best match for the search. You keep the winner live at its address, and you change the other pages around it.

Merge the Weaker Page Into the Stronger One

You merge pages when two pages answer the same question with the same depth. You move the best lines from the weaker page into the winner, and you delete the weaker page after the move. You redirect the old address to the winner, and the redirect passes the old links to the new home. A merge works best when the two pages share one topic.

Aim One Page at a Different Keyword

You retarget a page when it can serve a different search on its own, and you change the title, the first paragraph, and the headings so the page answers a new question. A page on cheap hotels in Bangkok can aim at family hotels in Bangkok instead, and the two pages stop fighting once each one owns its own phrase. You also change the internal links so they match each phrase, and the intent behind each phrase decides the match. You can learn that method in What Is Search Intent in SEO.

Use Internal Links to Point at the Winner

You use internal links to tell Google which page should win the phrase. You link from the weaker page to the winner with the target phrase as the anchor, and you remove links that point the other way. Google reads those links as votes, and the votes push the winner higher. Strong internal links lift the winner, and you can read the full method in What Is Topical Authority in SEO and How Do You Build It?.

How You Stop It From Coming Back

You stop the fight from coming back by planning each new page before you write it. You check your site for the target phrase first, and you write a new page only when no live page owns that phrase. You keep a simple list of your pages and their target phrases, and you read the list before each new post. That list takes minutes to keep, and it saves hours of fixes later.

A food blog keeps a sheet that lists every recipe and its target phrase. A writer who plans a new soup recipe checks the sheet first, and the writer finds two soup pages already live. The writer aims the new recipe at a fresh phrase instead, and the site gains a new ranking page rather than a new fight.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is keyword cannibalisation in SEO?

Keyword cannibalisation in SEO happens when two or more pages on your site target the same search phrase. Google cannot pick a clear winner, so the pages split clicks, links, and ranking strength. One strong page would rank higher than two pages that fight each other.

How do I know if my site has keyword cannibalisation?

You open Search Console, filter the performance report by one query, and read the pages that appear for it. Two or more pages on the same query means those pages compete. You repeat the check for your best queries, and you note every query that shows two pages.

Is keyword cannibalisation always bad for SEO?

No, two pages can cover related topics without harm when each one answers a different question. A guide and a service page can live side by side when each one targets its own phrase. The harm starts only when both pages chase the same phrase with the same answer.

Should I delete the weaker page or redirect it?

You redirect the weaker page when it holds links or traffic that you want to keep. You delete it without a redirect only when no one links to it and no one visits it. A redirect passes the old links to the winner, and a plain delete loses them, so you check the weaker page in Search Console before you choose.

Can I fix cannibalisation without deleting any page?

Yes, you retarget one page at a different phrase by changing its title, first paragraph, and headings. You also change the internal links so each page points at the phrase it owns. The fix works when the retargeted page holds enough fresh detail for its new phrase.

How long does it take for rankings to recover after a fix?

Recovery time varies by site, and Google must recrawl the changed pages before it updates the results. You ask Google to recrawl the winner through Search Console after a merge or a redirect. You watch the query in the performance report over the next weeks, and you judge the fix by steady clicks rather than daily moves.

Does keyword cannibalisation affect new websites?

Yes, new sites often publish several thin posts on one phrase in their first months, and the posts fight from the start. A new site gains more from one deep page than from five thin pages on the same phrase. You plan each new page against your list of live target phrases, and you skip a phrase that a live page already owns.

What is the difference between keyword cannibalisation and duplicate content?

Cannibalisation means two pages chase the same phrase with different words, and duplicate content means two pages hold the same words. Google treats both as a sign that your site lacks one clear answer. You fix cannibalisation by merging or retargeting, and you fix duplicate content by merging or removing one copy.
