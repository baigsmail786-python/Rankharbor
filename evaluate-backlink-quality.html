How to Evaluate Backlink Quality: A 9-Point Checklist

Domain metrics alone do not make a link good. Use this nine-point checklist — relevance, traffic signals, editorial standards, link patterns and more — before you accept any placement.

Why metrics alone mislead

A single authority score is the easiest number to game and the least reliable way to judge a link. Sites with impressive metrics can be link farms in disguise, while a small niche publication with modest metrics can send a link worth ten of theirs. Evaluate the whole picture.

The 9-point checklist

Work through these checks for every candidate placement:

  • 1. Topical relevance — does the site (and the specific page) cover your niche or your buyers' world?
  • 2. Real audience — are there signs of genuine readership: engagement, an editorial voice, a real team?
  • 3. Traffic signals — does the site appear to receive organic traffic for relevant topics?
  • 4. Editorial standards — would this article exist if nobody paid for links?
  • 5. Outbound link patterns — do articles link naturally to varied sources, or sell a link in every post?
  • 6. Indexation — is the linking page indexed and capable of ranking?
  • 7. Anchor-text profile — does the site's outbound anchor text look natural or stuffed?
  • 8. Site health — no hacked pages, spun content, or casino/pharma neighbours in the link profile
  • 9. Placement context — will your link sit in the main content, read by humans, not in a footer or author-box farm?

Red flags that end the conversation

Walk away from sites that publish on any topic for a fee, guarantee placement without editorial review, show thousands of unrelated outbound links, or cannot show you real published examples. Also be wary of brand-new sites with suspiciously perfect metrics.

Applying the checklist in practice

Score each candidate against the nine points and record the reasoning. In our campaigns, this evaluation happens before a publisher is ever proposed to a client — see how we evaluate publishers on the SaaS link building page. A smaller list of placements that pass all nine checks will outperform a larger list that passes three.

How to check relevance in five minutes

Relevance is the first check because a link from an unrelated site rarely helps, however strong the site looks. Start with the linking site's homepage and its recent articles. Does it cover your industry, your buyers' problems, or a closely related field? A project-management tool earns a relevant link from a site about running a small team, but not from a site about car parts, even if the car site has impressive metrics. Then check the specific page, not just the site. The page should discuss a topic your target page genuinely relates to, so a reader can see why the link is there.

How to read traffic and link patterns

Traffic signals are useful, but only when you read them with care. A site with steady organic traffic for topics in your field is a good sign. A site with traffic only for unrelated or strange keywords is a warning sign, even if the total number looks large. Look at which pages bring the traffic. If the top pages are genuine guides that rank for real questions, the site has an audience. If the top pages are thin posts stuffed with keywords, the traffic may not last, and neither will the value of your link.

How to keep a simple evaluation record

Write down your evaluation for every placement you accept or reject. A simple sheet with the site, the page, your score on each of the nine checks, and one line explaining the decision is enough. This record does three useful things. First, it stops you from re-checking the same site twice. Second, it shows patterns over time: you will notice which kinds of sites keep passing and which keep failing. Third, it protects you when a placement goes wrong, because you can see exactly what you checked and what changed.

What to do with a borderline link

Not every candidate is clearly good or clearly bad. A small blog with a real author and a modest audience might be relevant but new. A larger site might be relevant but link out heavily. For borderline cases, ask one question: would you be happy for a customer to see this link? If the answer is yes, the placement is probably fine, especially if the anchor text is natural and the target page is genuinely useful. If the answer is no, or you would rather not mention the site to anyone, walk away.
