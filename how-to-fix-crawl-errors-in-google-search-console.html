How to Fix Crawl Errors in Google Search Console

Crawl errors stop Google from reading your pages. Learn what each error means in Search Console and how to fix 404s, server errors, and redirect chains step by step.

Short answer: You fix crawl errors in Google Search Console by opening the Pages report, sorting the errors by type, and fixing the cause on your own site first. Most errors come from dead links, bad redirects, or server faults. You fix the cause, and then you ask Google to validate the fix.

Most site owners stare at the error list and fix the wrong thing first. They chase small warnings while a broken redirect blocks their best page from search results.

You will learn how to read each error type, fix the cause behind it, and confirm that Google sees the fix.

What Crawl Errors Mean in Search Console

A crawl error means Google tried to fetch a page and failed to read it cleanly. Search Console groups these failures under the Pages report, and it labels each one with a cause. The label tells you what went wrong, and it tells you where to start the fix. You read the label before you touch anything, and you never guess the cause from the count alone.

This article explains how to fix crawl errors in Google Search Console for a site of any size. The steps stay the same for a ten page blog and a ten thousand page store. You sort by error type, fix the largest group first, and validate the fix inside the same report. That order saves hours, and it keeps your best pages visible while you work.

A single error rarely harms a whole site, and a pattern of errors does real harm. Ten dead links on one old post matter little, and a thousand dead links across a store point to a broken migration. You judge the pattern, and you fix the pattern rather than one URL at a time.

How You Fix 404 Errors That Hurt Real Pages

A 404 error means the page no longer exists at that address. You fix it by deciding what should live at that address now. A page that moved gets a redirect to its new home, and a page that never should have existed gets left alone. You never redirect every dead page to the homepage, because that move confuses readers and wastes the old link value.

A small shop moved its product pages to new addresses and forgot the redirects. Search Console showed 80 new 404 errors in one week, and sales from search fell the same week. The owner mapped each old address to its new product page, and the errors cleared within two weeks. The sales recovered with the errors, and the owner learned to map redirects before any future move.

You also fix the internal links that point to dead pages on your own site. A menu link to a deleted page sends readers into an error, and it tells Google that your site lacks care. You run a link checker across your site each month, and you update every internal link that returns a 404. That monthly check keeps new errors from piling up What Is Robots.txt and How Does It Work.

How You Fix Server Errors and Redirect Chains

A server error means your server failed to answer Google when it asked for the page. You check the server first, and you check the page second. A short outage creates a few errors that clear on their own, and a repeated error points to a real fault in your code or your hosting. You read the server log for the exact time of the error, and you fix the fault that the log names.

Fix 5xx Errors at the Source

A 5xx error means the server broke while it tried to build the page. Heavy plugins, broken database calls, and full disk space cause most of these errors. You disable the newest plugin first, and you test the page again after each change. A page that loads for you but fails for Google often fails under load, and you test it with a fresh cache to see the same failure.

Break Long Redirect Chains

A redirect chain means one address points to a second address that points to a third. Google follows a few hops and then stops, and the final page never gets read. You shorten every chain to one hop from the old address to the final address. A chain of four hops on a category page cost one store its rank for a month, and a single direct redirect restored it in days.

People who learn how to fix crawl errors in Google Search Console often fix the page and forget the chain behind it. You list every redirect on the path, and you replace the whole chain with one direct redirect. That single change lets Google reach the page, and it lets readers reach it faster too How Does Google Crawl and Index a Website.

How You Confirm the Fix and Keep Errors Low

You validate the fix inside Search Console after you change the site. You open the error group, and you click the button that asks Google to check the fix. Google recrawls the sample URLs, and it marks the group as passed when the errors clear. You wait for that verdict, and you never assume the fix worked because the page loads for you.

You also set a monthly routine that catches new errors early. You open the Pages report on the first Monday of each month, and you note the new errors since your last check. You fix the largest group first, and you validate it the same day. That routine keeps the error count low, and it stops small faults from growing into a site wide problem.

A Calm Routine Beats a Panic Fix

Crawl errors look scary in a long list, and they shrink fast once you sort them by type. You fix the cause on your site, validate the fix in Search Console, and check again next month. Your pages stay readable, and Google keeps finding them without a fight. You open the report on the first Monday of each month, and you fix the largest group the same day.

When someone asks you how to fix crawl errors in Google Search Console, you can name the error type, the cause behind it, and the validation step that proves the fix. That answer shows real skill, and it keeps the site healthy for the long term. You keep the routine monthly, and the error list stays short.

Frequently Asked Questions

How to fix crawl errors in Google Search Console?

You open the Pages report, sort the errors by type, and fix the cause on your own site first. Most errors come from dead links, bad redirects, or server faults. You fix the cause, and then you click validate fix so Google recrawls the sample URLs and confirms the result.

What causes crawl errors?

Crawl errors come from dead pages, bad redirects, server faults, and blocked resources. A page that moved without a redirect creates a 404 error. A server that fails under load creates a 5xx error. A long redirect chain stops Google before it reaches the final page. You read the error label to find the cause.

How long does Google take to validate a fix?

Google usually needs days to recrawl the sample URLs and mark the group as passed. Large sites may wait longer because Google recrawls them in batches. You check the validation status after a few days, and you fix any URLs that still fail. You never assume the fix worked without that verdict.

Should I fix every 404 error?

No. You fix 404 errors on pages that hold links, traffic, or value for readers. You leave alone the dead addresses that no one links to and no one visits. You redirect a moved page to its closest live page, and you never redirect every dead page to the homepage.

What is a soft 404 error?

A soft 404 means the page returns a success code yet holds almost no content. Google treats it as a dead page because it serves no reader. A thin category page with no products earns that label often. You add real content to the page, or you remove it and redirect it to a useful page.

What is a 5xx server error in Search Console?

A 5xx error means your server failed while it tried to build the page. Heavy plugins, broken database calls, and full disk space cause most of these errors. You read the server log for the exact time of the error, and you fix the fault that the log names. A short outage clears on its own after the next crawl.

How do I find which pages link to a 404 page?

Search Console shows the referring pages for many 404 errors when you open a single URL in the report. Your own link checker also lists internal links that point to dead pages. You fix those internal links first, and you redirect the dead page when outside sites still link to it.

Can crawl errors hurt my rankings?

Yes. Errors on valuable pages stop Google from reading them, and unread pages cannot rank. A pattern of errors across a site also signals poor care, and it wastes the crawl time Google gives you. You fix errors on valuable pages first, and you keep a monthly routine that catches new ones early.
