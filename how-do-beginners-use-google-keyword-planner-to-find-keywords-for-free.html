How Do Beginners Use Google Keyword Planner to Find Keywords for Free?

Use Google Keyword Planner for free to find keywords. Set it up without an ad, read volume and competition, and pick one keyword to target.

Short answer: You learn how to use Google Keyword Planner for beginners by opening it in a free Google Ads account, choosing Discover new keywords, and typing a few seed words. The tool shows keyword ideas with volume and competition, and you pick one keyword to target.

Most beginners open the tool and drown in a thousand keywords, and they pick the biggest one without thinking. They then write a page they cannot rank, when a smaller keyword would bring real readers.

You will set the tool up without running an ad, and you will turn a long list into one target keyword. You will then write that page.

Set Up Keyword Planner Without Running an Ad

You set up Keyword Planner inside a Google Ads account, and you do not need to spend money on ads to use it. You sign in with your Google account, and you skip the campaign creation step when Google prompts you to build one. You switch the account to Expert mode, and you open the Tools menu to find Keyword Planner under Planning. A freelancer in Lahore set it up this way in ten minutes, and she has used it for free ever since.

Sign In and Skip the Campaign

You visit the Google Ads home page, and you sign in with the Google account you want to use. Google walks you through setting up your first ad, and you click the skip option instead of building a campaign. You may need to add billing details to reach the tool, and you pause any campaign that appears so it never spends. You open the Tools menu, and you click Keyword Planner under the Planning column.

Switch to Expert Mode First

You switch the account to Expert mode before you start, because Smart mode hides the tools you need. You find the mode switch in the account settings, and you confirm the change once. You check that the Tools menu shows Keyword Planner, and you open it to see the two options inside. You keep the account in Expert mode, and the tool stays available whenever you need it.

Discover New Keywords From Seed Words

You learn how to use Google Keyword Planner for beginners fastest when you start with a few seed words and let the tool suggest the rest. You click Discover new keywords, and you type three to five words that describe your topic. You can also enter your own website address, and the tool scans the page for keyword ideas. You click Get results, and the tool shows you a list of ideas with volume and competition for each one.

Start With Keywords You Already Know

You type a few seed keywords that describe your business, such as wedding cakes or local SEO, and the tool suggests related terms you never thought of. You keep the seed words short, and you let the tool do the expanding. You add the website option when you want ideas from a competitor page, and you compare the two lists side by side. You save the ideas that match your topic, and you ignore the ones that belong to another business.

Start With a Website for Competitor Ideas

You enter a competitor website address, and the tool scans the page for the keywords it covers. You read the ideas it returns, and you note the ones your own site could answer better. You never copy a competitor page, because your page must earn its place with better content. You use the list to find gaps, and you pick the keyword where your knowledge runs deepest.

Read Volume Competition and Intent Before You Pick

You read the results table before you pick, because the columns tell you which keyword is worth your time. You read the average monthly searches column to see how many people search the term, and you read the competition column to see how many advertisers bid on it. You filter by location and language, so the list matches the readers you actually serve. A small agency in Karachi filters every list to Pakistan first, and its picks match its real customers.

Volume Tells You the Size of the Prize

You read the volume column as a range, because the tool shows broad ranges for free accounts and not exact counts. You treat a high volume keyword as a big prize with big competition, and a low volume keyword as a small prize you can actually win. You pick a keyword with enough volume to matter and low enough competition to win. You never pick a keyword on volume alone, because a big number means nothing without the right intent.

Competition and Intent Tell You the Real Cost

You read the competition column to see how hard advertisers fight for the term, and you treat high competition as a warning for a new site. You read the intent behind the keyword, and you ask whether the searcher wants to learn, compare, or buy. You pick a keyword where the intent matches the page you can build, and you skip the ones that need a page you cannot write. You check the top results for the keyword, and you confirm that your page can beat them.

Turn a Long List Into One Target Keyword

You turn a long list into one target keyword with a simple pick rule, because a list of a thousand ideas is useless until you choose one. You sort the list by relevance first, and you remove every keyword that does not match your topic. You sort the rest by volume, and you remove every keyword with high competition. Export the List and Shortlist Five

You click the download button to export the full list as a spreadsheet, and you work through it away from the tool. You mark each keyword as yes, no, or maybe, and you keep only the yes rows. You sort the yes rows by volume, and you keep the top five. You check the top results for each of the five, and you pick the one with the weakest top pages.

Pick the Keyword You Can Answer Best

You pick the keyword where your knowledge runs deepest, because a thin answer never wins a search. You ask yourself what you know about the topic, and you choose the keyword where you can add something new. You check that the keyword fits one page you can build, and you write the page to answer it fully. Frequently Asked Questions

What is Google Keyword Planner?

Google Keyword Planner is a free keyword research tool inside Google Ads, and it shows keyword ideas with volume and competition. You use it to find what people search for, and to pick the keywords your pages should target. You need a Google Ads account to open it, and you do not need to run an ad.

Is Google Keyword Planner free to use?

Google Keyword Planner is free to use, and you do not need to spend money on ads to open it. You need a Google Ads account, and you may need to add billing details to reach the tool. You skip the campaign creation step, and you pause any campaign that appears so it never spends.

What is the difference between Discover new keywords and Get search volume?

Discover new keywords finds fresh keyword ideas from seed words or a website, and it shows volume and competition for each one. Get search volume and forecasts checks a list of keywords you already have, and it shows their history and future trends. You use Discover first to find ideas, and you use Get search volume to check a shortlist.

How do I read search volume in Keyword Planner?

You read the average monthly searches column, and you treat it as a range because free accounts see broad ranges and not exact counts. You use the range to compare keywords, and you pick one with enough volume to matter. You never pick a keyword on volume alone, because intent and competition matter just as much.

What does competition mean in Keyword Planner?

Competition in Keyword Planner shows how many advertisers bid on the keyword, and it tells you how hard the term is to win in ads. You treat high competition as a warning for a new site, and you look for lower competition terms. You check the organic top results too, because ad competition and organic competition are not the same.

Can I use Keyword Planner for SEO and not ads?

You can use Keyword Planner for SEO, and many beginners do exactly that. You use Discover new keywords to find ideas, and you read volume and competition to pick the ones a new page can win. You ignore the bidding columns, because they matter for ads and not for organic search.
