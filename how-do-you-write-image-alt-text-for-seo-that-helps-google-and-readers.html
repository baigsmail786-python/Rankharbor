How Do You Write Image Alt Text for SEO That Helps Google and Readers?

Learn to write image alt text for SEO that helps Google and readers. See good and bad examples, the 125 character guide, and when to leave alt text empty.

Short answer: Alt text is the short description you write in the alt attribute of an image, and it tells Google and screen readers what the image shows. You learn how to write image alt text for SEO by describing the image purpose in one plain sentence.

Most beginners stuff keywords into every alt text or leave it blank and hope Google guesses. Keyword stuffing reads badly, and blank alt text tells Google nothing at all. Both habits waste the ranking help that good alt text gives.

You finish able to write alt text for any image in a minute and check a whole article in five.

Learn What Alt Text Does for Google and Readers

Alt text lives inside the image code, and it shows on the page only when the image fails to load. Screen readers read it aloud to blind readers, while Google reads it to learn what the image shows. A photo of a chocolate cake with clear alt text helps Google match the page to cake searches. That one line serves readers, search engines, and image search at once.

Screen Readers Speak Your Alt Text Aloud

A blind reader hears your alt text through a screen reader, so the words should paint the picture in plain words. A photo of a baker icing a cake needs alt text that says a baker ices a chocolate cake in a bright kitchen. A vague line that says image tells the reader nothing, while a clear line lets the reader follow the article. Write alt text for the reader first, and the SEO help follows.

Google Reads Alt Text to Place Images in Search

Google cannot see a photo the way a person sees it, so it reads the alt text, the file name, and the words around the image. A product photo with alt text that names the product and its colour can rank in Google Images for that product. A photo with blank alt text stays invisible to image search, even when the page ranks well. That gap explains why one clear line can bring a page extra visits from image search.

Write Alt Text With One Simple Formula

Good alt text follows one simple shape: name the subject, add one key detail, and add the context of the page. A photo of a red bicycle on a city street needs alt text that says a red bicycle parked on a city street near a cafe. That line names the subject, the colour, and the place in one breath. A beginner who follows that shape writes strong alt text for almost any image.

Describe the Purpose, Not the Pixels

Alt text should say what the image does on the page, not list every pixel in the frame. A chart that shows sales rising needs alt text that says a chart shows sales rising from January to June. A list of colours and bars helps nobody, while the trend in one line helps every reader. Ask what the image adds to the article, and write that answer as the alt text.

Keep It Short and Add the Keyword Only When It Fits

Alt text works best under 125 characters, because screen readers cut longer lines and readers lose the point. A short line also forces you to keep the words that matter and drop the rest. Add the keyword only when the image truly shows it, such as a photo of the exact product the page sells. Use One Starter Set for a Whole Article

A single article holds a few kinds of images, and each kind needs a different line of alt text. The featured image sets the topic, a screenshot shows a step, and a chart shows a result. A beginner who writes one line for each kind covers the whole article in minutes. That starter set turns alt text from a chore into a quick habit.

Write Three Lines for One Article

A blog article with three images needs three lines, one for each kind. The featured image gets a line that names the topic, such as a baker icing a chocolate cake in a bright kitchen. A screenshot gets a line that names the step it shows, such as the WordPress editor with the publish button circled. A chart gets a line that names the result, such as a chart shows clicks rising from January to June.

Leave Alt Text Empty for Decorative Images

A decorative image adds beauty yet carries no message, so its alt text should stay empty. A thin border, a soft background shape, or a small icon beside a heading needs no words at all. An empty alt attribute tells the screen reader to skip the image, so the blind reader hears only the words that matter. You learn how to write image alt text for SEO when you know which images deserve words and which deserve silence.

Check the Alt Text of a New Article in Five Minutes

A new article needs one quick alt text check before it goes live, and the check takes five minutes. Open the article, read each image, and ask whether the alt text names what the image shows. A WordPress.com free site lets you add alt text in the image settings without any plugin. That short check keeps every article clean from the first day.

Run the Five Minute Check on Every Image

Read the alt text of each image aloud and ask whether a blind reader would picture the image from those words. A line that names the subject and one detail passes the test, while a line that says image fails it at once. Fix each weak line before you publish, because a fix after publishing takes the same minute yet helps fewer readers. That habit keeps the whole site clean without a crawler or a paid tool.

Alt Text, Caption, and File Name Each Do a Job

Alt text describes the image for readers and search engines, a caption adds a note that all readers can see, and the file name helps Google sort the image. A photo named chocolate cake recipe with alt text that describes the cake gives Google three matching signals. A photo named IMG 1234 with blank alt text gives Google nothing to read. Frequently Asked Questions

How long should alt text be?

Keep alt text under 125 characters, because screen readers cut longer lines and readers lose the point. A short line also forces you to keep the words that matter. Most strong alt text runs one plain sentence.

Should every image have alt text?

No, decorative images should carry empty alt text so screen readers skip them. A thin border or a small icon needs no words at all. Every image that carries a message needs a clear line.

Can I put keywords in alt text?

Add the keyword only when the image truly shows it, such as a photo of the exact product the page sells. Keyword stuffing reads badly and helps no ranking. A natural line that names the subject beats a stuffed line every time.

What is the difference between alt text and a caption?

Alt text hides in the image code and serves screen readers and search engines, while a caption shows beside the image for all readers to see. A caption adds a note or a credit, while alt text describes the image itself. A page can use both at once.

What makes good alt text?

Good alt text names the subject, adds one key detail, and adds the context of the page in one plain sentence. A photo of a red bicycle on a city street needs a line that says just that. Anyone who asks how to write image alt text for SEO should start with that simple shape.

What is bad alt text?

Bad alt text says image, photo, or a string of keywords with no meaning. A line that says image tells the reader nothing, while a stuffed line reads badly to everyone. Write the line for a blind reader first, and the SEO help follows.

Does alt text help my page rank on Google?

Yes, alt text helps Google learn what an image shows, and it can help the image rank in Google Images. A page with clear alt text also matches more searches than a page with blank alt text. The help stays small, yet it adds up across a whole site.

How do I add alt text in WordPress?

Open the image in the editor, open the image settings, and type the line in the alt text box. WordPress.com free sites offer the same box without any plugin. Anyone who learns how to write image alt text for SEO can check the box on every image before publishing.
