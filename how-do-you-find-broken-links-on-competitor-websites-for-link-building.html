How Do You Find Broken Links on Competitor Websites for Link Building?

Broken links on rival pages can earn you links from trusted sites. Learn how to list rival pages, run a link checker, and pitch a replacement that owners accept.

Short answer: You find broken links on competitor websites by listing the pages that link to your rivals and running a link checker over them. The checker flags the dead links, and you note the ones that match your content.

Most beginners scan their own site for broken links and miss the dead links on rival pages. Their own dead links earn nothing, while a rival dead link can earn a link from a trusted page. The search starts in the wrong place.

You will learn how to find broken links on competitor websites, run the checker, and pitch the replacement so the owner says yes.

Start With the Pages That Link to Your Rivals

Learning how to find broken links on competitor websites starts with a list of pages that link to your rivals. You open a backlink checker, type in a rival address, and export the list of pages that link to it. The list shows blogs, resource pages, and guides that already link out in your niche, and those pages form your hunting ground.

You sort the list by the strength of each linking page, and you keep the pages that still earn visits and still hold their own links. A resource page that ranks well makes a strong target, and a forgotten page with no visits makes a weak one. You keep the top 20 pages on your list, and you drop the rest for now.

A gardening blog listed the pages that linked to three rival gardening sites, and it found one university resource page with 14 outgoing links. The blog owned a fresh guide that covered the same ground as three of the dead pages on that list. The blog pitched all three replacements in one mail, and the university fixed two of them with links to the blog. The blog earned two links from one page, and it spent one afternoon on the whole job.

Run a Link Checker Over Those Pages

You run a free link checker over each page on your list, and the checker lists every outgoing link that returns an error. The checker flags 404 errors, server errors, and pages that moved without a redirect. You note each dead link next to the page that holds it, and you copy the dead address for your pitch.

You confirm each dead link by opening it in your browser before you pitch it. A checker can flag a link that works fine when a server blocks the checker, and a manual check catches that false alarm. You pitch only the links that fail in your own browser, and you pitch them on the same day you check them. A link that died last month may live again today, and a stale pitch marks you as careless.

A fitness site ran a checker over 30 rival pages and found nine dead links in one hour. The site checked each link by hand, and two of the nine loaded fine in a browser. The site pitched the seven real dead links, and three owners replied within a week. The two false alarms never reached an inbox, and the site kept its name clean.

Pick the Dead Links That Match Your Content

You pick a dead link only when your own page covers the same topic as the dead page. The replacement must match the dead page closely, or the site owner will ignore your pitch. You read the dead page in an archive copy when you can, and you shape your replacement to cover the same points. That extra reading lifts your reply rate, and it shows the owner that you did real work.

You skip dead links that point to topics you never cover, and you skip pages with no traffic and no links of their own. A link from a dead corner of the web helps no one, and a pitch for a mismatched topic wastes your time and the owner time. You chase dead links on pages that still earn visits and still hold their own links, because those links carry real value.

People who ask how to find broken links on competitor websites often collect a long list and pitch it all at once, and the mass pitch fails. You pick five dead links that match your best pages, and you pitch those five first. Five careful pitches beat 50 careless ones, and the replies teach you which pages earn links. Resource pages hold many of the dead links you chase, and you can learn that method in What Is Resource Page Link Building and How Does It Work?.

Pitch the Replacement So the Owner Says Yes

You write a short mail that names the exact broken link and the exact page that holds it. You state the error in plain words, and you offer your page as one option rather than a demand. Site owners read short mails first, and they delete long mails that praise their site in vague words. You keep your mail under 120 words, and you put the broken link in the first three lines.

You address the page author by name, and you find that name on the page itself. A mail to a generic inbox often dies unread, while a mail to the author reaches a human. You find the author name in two minutes, and that small step lifts your replies. Your replacement page must also read better than the dead page did, because the page does the real work and the mail only opens the door. A strong page earns the link, and you can learn the full link building method in How to Build Backlinks for a New Website Without a Budget.

A Habit That Earns Links Each Month

You build this search into a monthly habit, and the habit earns links while your rivals ignore their dead links. You list fresh rival pages each month, run the checker, and pitch the matches the same week. Your reply rate grows with each batch, and your link profile grows with it. The work takes one afternoon a month, and the links it earns last for years.

When someone asks you how to find broken links on competitor websites, you can describe the list, the checker, and the short mail in one breath. That proof beats any promise, and it earns links that last for years. You keep that proof in your outreach notes, and you reuse it in your next pitch.

Frequently Asked Questions

How to find broken links on competitor websites?

You list the pages that link to your rivals with a backlink checker, and you run a free link checker over those pages. The checker flags every dead link, and you note the ones that match content you already own. You confirm each dead link in your browser on the day you pitch it.

What tools find broken links on competitor pages?

Free link checkers scan a page for dead outgoing links, and browser extensions flag dead links as you browse. Backlink tools list the pages that link to your rivals, and Ahrefs and Semrush also report broken outgoing links in their site audits. You start with a free checker, and you pay only when you run large batches each week.

How do I find which sites link to my competitors?

You type a rival address into a backlink checker, and the tool lists every page that links to that rival. You sort the list by the strength of each linking page, and you keep the resource pages and guides that still earn visits. Those pages form your hunting ground for dead links.

What makes a broken link worth pitching?

A broken link deserves a pitch when your own page covers the same topic as the dead page, and when the page that holds the link still earns visits. The replacement must match the dead page closely, and it must read better than the dead page did. A link from a page with no traffic earns you almost nothing.

How do I check if a link is really broken?

You open the link in your own browser and read the result. A checker can flag a link that works fine when a server blocks the checker, and a manual check catches that false alarm. You pitch only the links that fail in your own browser, and you pitch them on the same day you check them.

How many broken link pitches should I send per day?

You send as many as you can research properly, and most beginners manage 10 to 20 good pitches a day. Each pitch needs a verified dead link, a matching replacement page, and the author name. You send fewer careful pitches rather than many careless ones, and your reply rate will show the difference.

What do I write in a broken link outreach email?

You name the exact broken link and the page that holds it in the first three lines. You state the error in plain words, and you offer your page as one option. You keep the mail under 120 words, and you address the page author by name. You never ask for a link before you name the problem you found.

Does finding broken links on competitor sites still work?

Yes. Sites still link out a lot, and pages still move or close, so dead links keep appearing. The method works when your replacement page matches the dead page closely and reads better than it did. It fails when you pitch thin pages or dead links you never verified.
