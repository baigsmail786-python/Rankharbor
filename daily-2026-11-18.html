Domain Authority vs Domain Rating: What Is the Difference and Which One Matters?

Category: SEO Basics • Keyword: domain authority vs domain rating

Domain authority and domain rating both predict ranking strength, and they measure different things. Learn what each score means, why the numbers disagree, and which one deserves your attention.

Short answer: Domain authority is a Moz score from 1 to 100, and domain rating is an Ahrefs score from 0 to 100. Both scores predict how well a site may rank, and both come from link data rather than from Google. Domain authority vs domain rating differs in the math behind each number.

Many beginners place the two numbers side by side and read them as one scale. The scales share a 1 to 100 range, and they measure different things with different link data. A direct comparison misleads the reader.

You will learn what each score measures, why the numbers disagree, and which score deserves your attention.

What Domain Authority Measures

Moz built domain authority to predict ranking strength across many sites, and Moz calculates it from link data and other signals. The score runs from 1 to 100, and new sites start near the bottom of that range. Strong sites with many trusted links sit near the top, and the score rises slowly as the link profile grows.

A local bakery site with 20 links from local blogs may hold a score near 15. A national news site with links from thousands of trusted sites may hold a score near 90. The gap shows link strength, and it also shows age and size. You read the number as a rough guide to link strength, and you never read it as a Google rank.

One link from a respected university counts for more than 50 links from thin directory pages, and depth on one subject builds the same kind of trust. Moz updates the score as its link index changes, so your number moves when your link profile changes. You track the trend across months, and you ignore small moves from week to week. The steps that lift the score appear in What Is Domain Authority in SEO and How Do You Improve It?.

What Domain Rating Measures

Ahrefs built domain rating to measure the strength of a site link profile, and Ahrefs calculates it from the quality of the domains that link to the site. The score runs from 0 to 100, and it rises when strong domains link to the site. A site with links from a few powerful domains can outscore a site with links from hundreds of weak domains.

Domain rating weighs the linking domain rather than the raw link count, and that choice sets it apart from domain authority. A link from a strong news site lifts the score more than 100 links from new blogs. The score counts each linking domain once, so one domain gives one vote no matter how many pages it links from.

A small tool site earned links from three respected software blogs, and its domain rating jumped past sites with far more links. The three blogs held high scores of their own, and their links passed real strength to the small site. The example shows why one strong link beats a pile of weak ones, and why the raw count misleads.

Why the Two Scores Disagree on the Same Site

Domain authority vs domain rating splits beginners because the two scores disagree on the same site, and the split fades once you see the two causes. The scores read different link indexes and apply different math to the links they find. Moz and Ahrefs crawl the web on their own schedules, and each one finds a different set of links to your site. The same site can hold a domain authority of 35 and a domain rating of 52 on the same day, and neither number is wrong.

Different Link Indexes

Moz finds one set of links, Ahrefs finds another set, and neither crawler sees the whole web. A link that Moz missed still lifts your Ahrefs score, and a link that Ahrefs missed still lifts your Moz score. The gap between the two numbers often shows the gap between the two indexes rather than a change in your site. You treat each score as a view from one tool, and you never mix the two views into one number.

Different Math Behind Each Number

Domain authority blends link counts with link quality and other signals into one number. Domain rating weighs the strength of the linking domains and the number of unique domains. A site with many links from weak domains can hold a fair domain authority and a low domain rating at the same time. The math explains the gap, and the gap tells you which kind of links you lack. Deep coverage of one subject earns the strong links that lift both scores, and you can learn that method in What Is Topical Authority in SEO and How Do You Build It?.

Which Score Matters for Your Work

When you weigh domain authority vs domain rating for your own tracking, one score matters as a tool and neither matters as a target. You pick the tool that your team already uses, and you track that one score each month. A rising trend shows that your links grow stronger, and a flat trend shows that your link work needs fresh effort.

You compare your score with direct rivals inside the same tool, and you set your target above theirs. A score of 25 can lead a small niche when every rival sits below 20, and a score of 40 can lag in a crowded niche when rivals sit above 60. The rival gap decides your target, and the tool you pick decides the number you watch.

You also pair the score with traffic, leads, and ranking pages before you judge any progress. A rising score with no new ranking pages points to links that bring no traffic. A flat score with new ranking pages shows that your content does the heavy work, and the score follows the content in time.

A Simple Way to Use Both Scores

Domain authority vs domain rating is a choice between two views of the same link profile, and you need only one view for daily work. You pick one tool, track its score each month, and compare it with rivals inside that same tool. The number guides your link work, and it never replaces traffic and leads as the real proof of progress.

You start this month by recording both scores next to your link count and your ranking pages. You earn links from real sites, fix weak pages, and publish guides that help readers. You check both scores next month, and you keep going when the trend points up.

How Do You Find Broken Links on Competitor Websites for Link Building?

Most beginners scan their own site for broken links and miss the dead links on rival pages. Their own dead links earn nothing, while a rival dead link can earn a link from a trusted page. The search starts in the wrong place.

Start With the Pages That Link to Your Rivals

Learning how to find broken links on competitor websites starts with a list of pages that link to your rivals. You open a backlink checker, type in a rival address, and export the list of pages that link to it. The list shows blogs, resource pages, and guides that already link out in your niche, and those pages form your hunting ground.

You sort the list by the strength of each linking page, and you keep the pages that still earn visits and still hold their own links. A resource page that ranks well makes a strong target, and a forgotten page with no visits makes a weak one. You keep the top 20 pages on your list, and you drop the rest for now.

A gardening blog listed the pages that linked to three rival gardening sites, and it found one university resource page with 14 outgoing links. The blog owned a fresh guide that covered the same ground as three of the dead pages on that list. The blog pitched all three replacements in one mail, and the university fixed two of them with links to the blog. The blog earned two links from one page, and it spent one afternoon on the whole job.

Run a Link Checker Over Those Pages

You run a free link checker over each page on your list, and the checker lists every outgoing link that returns an error. The checker flags 404 errors, server errors, and pages that moved without a redirect. You note each dead link next to the page that holds it, and you copy the dead address for your pitch.

You confirm each dead link by opening it in your browser before you pitch it. A checker can flag a link that works fine when a server blocks the checker, and a manual check catches that false alarm. You pitch only the links that fail in your own browser, and you pitch them on the same day you check them. A link that died last month may live again today, and a stale pitch marks you as careless.

A fitness site ran a checker over 30 rival pages and found nine dead links in one hour. The site checked each link by hand, and two of the nine loaded fine in a browser. The site pitched the seven real dead links, and three owners replied within a week. The two false alarms never reached an inbox, and the site kept its name clean.

Pick the Dead Links That Match Your Content

You pick a dead link only when your own page covers the same topic as the dead page. The replacement must match the dead page closely, or the site owner will ignore your pitch. You read the dead page in an archive copy when you can, and you shape your replacement to cover the same points. That extra reading lifts your reply rate, and it shows the owner that you did real work.

You skip dead links that point to topics you never cover, and you skip pages with no traffic and no links of their own. A link from a dead corner of the web helps no one, and a pitch for a mismatched topic wastes your time and the owner time. You chase dead links on pages that still earn visits and still hold their own links, because those links carry real value.

People who ask how to find broken links on competitor websites often collect a long list and pitch it all at once, and the mass pitch fails. You pick five dead links that match your best pages, and you pitch those five first. Five careful pitches beat 50 careless ones, and the replies teach you which pages earn links. Pitch the Replacement So the Owner Says Yes

You write a short mail that names the exact broken link and the exact page that holds it. You state the error in plain words, and you offer your page as one option rather than a demand. Site owners read short mails first, and they delete long mails that praise their site in vague words. You keep your mail under 120 words, and you put the broken link in the first three lines.

You address the page author by name, and you find that name on the page itself. A mail to a generic inbox often dies unread, while a mail to the author reaches a human. You find the author name in two minutes, and that small step lifts your replies. Your replacement page must also read better than the dead page did, because the page does the real work and the mail only opens the door. A Habit That Earns Links Each Month

You build this search into a monthly habit, and the habit earns links while your rivals ignore their dead links. You list fresh rival pages each month, run the checker, and pitch the matches the same week. Your reply rate grows with each batch, and your link profile grows with it. How to Fix Crawl Errors in Google Search Console

What Crawl Errors Mean in Search Console

A crawl error means Google tried to fetch a page and failed to read it cleanly. Search Console groups these failures under the Pages report, and it labels each one with a cause. The label tells you what went wrong, and it tells you where to start the fix. You read the label before you touch anything, and you never guess the cause from the count alone.

This article explains how to fix crawl errors in Google Search Console for a site of any size. The steps stay the same for a ten page blog and a ten thousand page store. You sort by error type, fix the largest group first, and validate the fix inside the same report. That order saves hours, and it keeps your best pages visible while you work.

A single error rarely harms a whole site, and a pattern of errors does real harm. Ten dead links on one old post matter little, and a thousand dead links across a store point to a broken migration. You judge the pattern, and you fix the pattern rather than one URL at a time.

How You Fix 404 Errors That Hurt Real Pages

A 404 error means the page no longer exists at that address. You fix it by deciding what should live at that address now. A page that moved gets a redirect to its new home, and a page that never should have existed gets left alone. You never redirect every dead page to the homepage, because that move confuses readers and wastes the old link value.

A small shop moved its product pages to new addresses and forgot the redirects. Search Console showed 80 new 404 errors in one week, and sales from search fell the same week. The owner mapped each old address to its new product page, and the errors cleared within two weeks. The sales recovered with the errors, and the owner learned to map redirects before any future move.

You also fix the internal links that point to dead pages on your own site. A menu link to a deleted page sends readers into an error, and it tells Google that your site lacks care. You run a link checker across your site each month, and you update every internal link that returns a 404. That monthly check keeps new errors from piling up What Is Robots.txt and How Does It Work.

How You Fix Server Errors and Redirect Chains

A server error means your server failed to answer Google when it asked for the page. You check the server first, and you check the page second. A short outage creates a few errors that clear on their own, and a repeated error points to a real fault in your code or your hosting. You read the server log for the exact time of the error, and you fix the fault that the log names.

Fix 5xx Errors at the Source

A 5xx error means the server broke while it tried to build the page. Heavy plugins, broken database calls, and full disk space cause most of these errors. You disable the newest plugin first, and you test the page again after each change. A page that loads for you but fails for Google often fails under load, and you test it with a fresh cache to see the same failure.

Break Long Redirect Chains

A redirect chain means one address points to a second address that points to a third. Google follows a few hops and then stops, and the final page never gets read. You shorten every chain to one hop from the old address to the final address. A chain of four hops on a category page cost one store its rank for a month, and a single direct redirect restored it in days.

People who learn how to fix crawl errors in Google Search Console often fix the page and forget the chain behind it. You list every redirect on the path, and you replace the whole chain with one direct redirect. How You Confirm the Fix and Keep Errors Low

You validate the fix inside Search Console after you change the site. You open the error group, and you click the button that asks Google to check the fix. Google recrawls the sample URLs, and it marks the group as passed when the errors clear. You wait for that verdict, and you never assume the fix worked because the page loads for you.

You also set a monthly routine that catches new errors early. You open the Pages report on the first Monday of each month, and you note the new errors since your last check. You fix the largest group first, and you validate it the same day. That routine keeps the error count low, and it stops small faults from growing into a site wide problem.
