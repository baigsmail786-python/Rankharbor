SEO Audit Checklist for Your First Website Review

Category: SEO Basics

Primary keyword: seo audit checklist

Meta description: Learn how to run a clear first website audit with a simple checklist that covers technical health and content quality and priority fixes.

You run a seo audit checklist across your whole site in one focused afternoon. You check technical health and content quality and internal links in one pass. You leave the session with a short fix list that you can act on this week.

Most beginners open a tool and chase every warning on the screen. They fix low value warnings first and they ignore broken pages that block real visitors. They end the day busy and their site still struggles.

You will finish with a clear order for fixes. You will know which pages need work first and which warnings you can ignore for now.

Check Technical Health Before You Touch Content

You start the audit by checking whether search engines can crawl and render your pages cleanly. You test your homepage and your key service pages and your top blog posts in a crawl tool. You note every page that returns an error and every page that loads slowly on a phone. This opening check gives you a reliable picture of site health before you judge any single article.

You check your robots file and your sitemap and your index coverage in the same session. You confirm that your sitemap lists live pages only and your robots file blocks no page that should rank. You fix any sitemap entry that points to a deleted page because dead entries waste crawl attention. sitemap setup guide

You test page speed on mobile for your five most visited pages. You compress large images and you remove unused scripts that delay the first paint. You retest after each change and you record the result in your audit notes. A faster page keeps visitors on the page longer and it gives search engines a cleaner page to evaluate.

You check mobile usability with your own phone and not only with a tool report. You tap every menu link and you complete one form and you read one article without zooming. You fix any button that sits too close to another button and any text block that overflows the screen. Real device checks catch problems that automated reports often miss.

Judge Content Quality Page by Page

You review your top twenty pages for clear purpose and clear answers. You ask one question for each page and you write the answer in one sentence. You flag any page that tries to cover three topics at once because mixed pages confuse readers and search engines. A focused page earns stronger relevance for one clear topic.

You compare each page against the search result it wants to win. You read the top results and you note the questions those pages answer directly. You add the missing answer to your page in your own words and you support it with a specific example. A bakery site used this method on its wedding cake page and it added clear pricing ranges and delivery areas and tasting booking steps. The page then matched buyer intent far better than a generic gallery page had done.

You look for thin pages that repeat the same short paragraph across many URLs. You merge near duplicate pages into one stronger page and you redirect the weaker URLs to the stronger page. You keep the best examples and the best explanations from each merged page. Consolidation strengthens one page instead of spreading weak signals across five pages.

You check internal links from each reviewed page to your next most relevant page. You add links where a reader naturally wants more detail and you use descriptive anchor text that names the destination topic. You avoid linking every page to your homepage because homepage links teach search engines nothing about topic relationships. Strong internal links guide readers deeper and they spread authority to pages that deserve more attention. internal linking strategy

Fix in Priority Order and Record Every Change

You sort your fix list by impact first and by effort second. You fix broken pages and blocked pages before you polish wording on a healthy page. You fix your homepage and your money pages before you fix low traffic archive pages. This order protects revenue pages first and it stops small tasks from eating your audit day.

You assign one owner and one deadline to every fix on the list. You write the fix in plain language so any team member can complete it without asking you for clarification. You mark each fix as done only after you recheck the live page. A seo audit checklist works only when completion means a live verified change and not a task you moved to done in a spreadsheet.

You schedule a short recheck two weeks after the first audit. You crawl the site again and you confirm that errors dropped and that redirects work. You compare traffic for fixed pages against the same period before the fix. You keep the comparison simple and you focus on clicks and impressions for the fixed pages.

You repeat the audit monthly with the same structure and the same notes format. You track recurring problems and you fix the root cause instead of patching the same symptom each month. A local clinic repeated this routine and it found the same broken booking link three months in a row until it fixed the template that generated the link. Monthly repetition turns audits from emergency cleanups into steady maintenance.

Conclusion

You now hold a simple audit routine that you can run without expensive tools. You check technical health and you judge content honestly and you fix in priority order. You repeat the routine monthly and your site grows stronger with each pass.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a first website audit take?

A first audit takes one focused afternoon for a small site with fewer than fifty pages. You spend the first hour on technical checks and the second hour on content review. You spend the final hour on priority fixes and notes. Larger sites need a full day because crawl data and content review both take longer. You can split a large audit across two days and still keep the same order.

Which free tools help a beginner run an audit?

Google Search Console shows index coverage and mobile usability and core web performance signals for your verified site. PageSpeed Insights tests speed on mobile and desktop and it names the largest delays. A free crawl tool scans your pages and it lists broken links and missing titles and redirect chains. You combine the three tools and you avoid buying a paid suite until your site grows past a few hundred pages.

How often should a small business audit its site?

A small business benefits from a full audit once each month and a quick check after any major change. You run a quick check after a redesign and after a migration and after you publish a large batch of pages. Monthly audits catch broken links early and they keep sitemaps clean. Quarterly audits leave too much time for errors to hurt traffic.

What counts as a priority fix during an audit?

A priority fix removes a barrier between a visitor and a goal or between a search engine and a page. Broken checkout pages and blocked key pages and missing contact forms all count as priority fixes. Slow images on a top landing page also count when they push visitors away. Cosmetic wording changes wait until priority fixes go live.

Should I fix every warning that an audit tool shows?

You should not fix every warning because tools flag many low value issues on healthy sites. You fix warnings that affect real pages and real visitors first. You ignore warnings about minor markup on archive pages when your money pages still carry errors. You revisit low value warnings only after priority fixes go live.

How do I audit a site that I did not build?

You start with access to Search Console and analytics and the content system. You crawl the site and you map the main page types and the main conversion paths. You interview the owner about the pages that bring enquiries and sales. You build your fix list around those pages and you document every assumption you made about the site structure.

What should I record after each audit?

You record the crawl date and the tool results and the pages you fixed and the pages that still need work. You record traffic for fixed pages before the fix and two weeks after the fix. You record recurring problems separately so you can spot template faults. Clear records turn each audit into a baseline for the next audit.

Can an audit alone improve search rankings?

An audit alone lifts rankings only when it removes real barriers to crawling and reading and converting. An audit that fixes broken pages and slow pages and thin pages gives search engines better pages to rank. An audit without fixes changes nothing because search engines rank the live site and not your notes. You pair every audit with fixes and a recheck to see real movement.

Digital PR Link Building for Editorial Links

Category: Link Building

Primary keyword: digital pr link building

Meta description: Learn digital PR link building with original stories and targeted journalist pitches that earn editorial links without paid placements.

Digital pr link building earns links when journalists cite your original insight in a published story. You create a story with real data or a clear expert view. You offer that story to the right journalist at the right time.

Most marketers pitch a product page and call the pitch digital PR. Journalists ignore product pitches because readers gain nothing from a sales page. Marketers then send more pitches and they damage their sender reputation with every batch.

You will finish with a repeatable story process. You will pitch fewer journalists and you will earn stronger editorial links.

Find a Story That Journalists Want to Cover

You start with a question that your audience already asks in public. You search forums and social posts and support tickets for repeated questions. You pick one question that carries a surprising answer or a clear conflict. Journalists cover tension and surprise and they ignore generic advice posts.

You gather original evidence for the story before you write any pitch. You run a small survey with your own users and you analyse public data and you ask an expert for a quotable view. You keep the dataset small and you check every number twice. Original evidence gives a journalist a reason to cite you instead of a competitor who only offers opinion.

You shape the story around one headline finding that a reader can grasp in seconds. You write the finding in plain language and you support it with two supporting facts. You avoid burying the finding in a long report because journalists skim pitches fast. A fitness app surveyed two hundred users about morning workout habits and it found that short sessions beat long sessions for consistency. News sites cited the finding because the result challenged a common belief.

You test the angle with one friendly editor or one industry peer before you pitch widely. You ask whether the story feels new and whether the evidence feels solid. You revise weak angles early and you save your media list for strong stories only. Testing protects your reputation and it raises your response rate over time. press outreach guide

Pitch the Right Journalist With a Clear Angle

You build a short media list for each story and you research each journalist before you contact them. You read their last five articles and you note the topics they cover and the sources they cite. You remove any journalist who covers a different beat because a wrong pitch wastes their time and your credibility. A focused list of twenty relevant journalists beats a blind list of two hundred contacts.

You write a subject line that names the finding and not your brand. You open the email with the finding and you add the supporting evidence in the next two sentences. You offer the full dataset and an expert quote and a chart in one short paragraph. Journalists reply faster when they see the story value before they see any request for a link.

You personalise each pitch with one specific reference to a recent article by that journalist. You connect your finding to their beat in one sentence and you explain why their readers care. You never send the same generic paragraph to every journalist because generic pitches signal low effort. A SaaS founder pitched a remote work journalist with a finding about async meeting habits and the journalist replied within one hour because the pitch matched her last three articles.

You follow a polite sequence and you stop after one thoughtful follow up. You send the follow up three days later with one new detail or one new quote. You thank journalists who decline and you keep them on your list for a future story that fits better. Respect builds long term relationships and long term relationships produce repeat coverage.

Turn Coverage Into Lasting Link Value

You track every published story and you check the link on the live page on the same day. You confirm that the link points to the right page and that the link passes value and that the anchor text describes your topic. You thank the journalist promptly and you share the story with your audience. Prompt thanks encourage future replies and they strengthen the relationship.

You capture coverage in a simple log with the outlet name and the article URL and the link target and the publish date. You review the log monthly and you note which story types earn the most links. You repeat the winning story types and you retire angles that earn no coverage after two attempts. A digital pr link building routine improves only when you measure story performance and not pitch volume.

You repurpose a successful story for a second wave without repeating the same pitch. You create a new chart or a regional breakdown or an expert reaction to the first coverage. You offer the new angle to journalists who did not cover the first story. Fresh angles extend the life of one dataset and they earn links from new outlets.

You protect link value by keeping the linked page live and relevant. You update the linked page when your data changes and you fix any redirect that breaks the link path. You never move a popular linked page without a redirect plan. A dead linked page wastes editorial trust and it loses the ranking benefit that the coverage created. link maintenance checklist

Conclusion

You now hold a story first method for earning editorial links. You find original evidence and you pitch relevant journalists and you protect every link you earn. You repeat the method with better stories each quarter and your link profile grows steadily.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is digital PR different from guest posting?

Digital PR earns a link when a journalist cites your story in news coverage. Guest posting earns a link when a site publishes your article on its blog. Digital PR usually reaches a larger audience and it carries stronger editorial trust. Guest posting gives you more control over anchor text and content. You can use both methods and you should match each method to a different goal.

How much original data do I need for a story?

You need enough data to support one headline finding and two supporting facts. A survey of one hundred relevant respondents can support a niche story. A public dataset with clear filters can support a broader story. You need transparent methods and a clear sample description so journalists can trust the result. Small clean data beats large messy data every time.

What makes a journalist reply to a pitch?

A journalist replies when the pitch matches their beat and offers a fresh finding fast. A clear subject line and a strong opening sentence raise reply rates. A short pitch with a dataset link and an expert quote saves the journalist research time. A generic pitch about your product rarely earns a reply because it offers no story value.

Should I pay for a link that a journalist offers?

You should not pay for a link in editorial coverage because paid links violate search engine guidelines. You risk a penalty and you risk damage to your brand when readers discover the payment. You offer value through data and expertise instead of payment. You decline paid placements politely and you keep the relationship open for genuine stories.

How long does a digital PR campaign take?

A small campaign takes three to four weeks from idea to first coverage. You spend one week on research and data and one week on story shaping and media list building. You spend one week on pitching and follow ups. Coverage often appears in the third or fourth week. Larger campaigns with surveys take six weeks because data collection takes longer.

Can a small business run digital PR without an agency?

A small business can run digital PR with one owner and one clear story per quarter. You start with local journalists and niche bloggers who cover your industry. You use your own customer data and your own expertise as story material. You keep pitches short and you track results in a simple sheet. Consistency matters more than budget for small campaigns.

What should I do when a journalist covers the story without a link?

You thank the journalist and you ask politely whether they can add a source link for readers. You explain that readers may want the full dataset and you share the exact URL. You accept a no gracefully and you keep the relationship intact. Many journalists add the link on request when the ask stays polite and brief.

How do I measure digital PR success?

You measure published stories and live links and referral traffic from each story. You also measure brand mentions even when a story carries no link. You compare link quality by outlet relevance and audience size. You track repeat coverage from the same journalists over time. Strong campaigns show steady growth in relevant coverage and not only one viral story.

SaaS Help Center SEO That Turns Support Pages Into Traffic

Category: SaaS SEO

Primary keyword: saas help center seo

Meta description: Learn SaaS help center SEO with question mapping and clear help page structure that turns support articles into search traffic and trials.

SaaS help center seo turns support pages into a steady source of search traffic. You optimise help articles for the exact questions that prospects type into search. You answer each question fully and you guide the reader toward a trial or a demo.

Most SaaS teams hide help content behind login walls or vague titles. Prospects search for a solution and they land on a competitor page that answers the question in public. Teams then lose trial signups to rivals who publish the same answers openly.

You will finish with a public help center that ranks for support questions. You will attract prospects at the moment they seek a solution.

Map the Questions Prospects Actually Search

You collect questions from support tickets and live chat logs and sales calls. You group repeated questions by task and you count how often each question appears. You prioritise questions that appear weekly because frequent questions signal steady search demand. Ticket data reveals real language and real language shapes search friendly titles.

You check search results for each top question and you note the pages that already rank. You study the headings on those pages and you note the steps they explain. You plan a better help article that covers the full task and not only one step. A project tool mapped fifty repeated setup questions and it discovered that setup questions drove more trial signups than feature announcement posts had driven.

You write titles that mirror the question in natural words. You use the task name and the product area in the title so readers recognise the match instantly. You avoid internal jargon in titles because prospects search with plain words. Clear titles raise click rates and they reduce support tickets for the same question.

You create a question bank in a simple sheet and you update it monthly with new ticket trends. You mark each question as published or planned or retired. You review retired questions when a feature changes because outdated answers hurt trust. A living question bank keeps your help center aligned with real user needs. keyword research for support content

Structure Help Pages So Search Engines Understand Them

You open each help article with a direct answer in the first two sentences. You state the outcome and you name the setting or the menu path that delivers it. Readers decide fast whether the page solves their problem and search engines read the same clear signal. Direct openings also power featured snippets for question queries.

You organise the article with clear headings for each step in the task. You write one task per heading and you keep each paragraph focused on one action. You add a short example after any step that confuses new users. A payroll SaaS rewrote its tax setup article with step headings and a worked example for one employee. Support tickets for that task dropped and the article started ranking for the setup question.

You add screenshots with descriptive file names and alt text that names the screen and the action. You compress each image before upload so help pages load fast on mobile. You caption any screenshot that shows a complex setting because captions guide readers who skim. Visual clarity helps new users complete tasks without contacting support.

You link each help article to two related help articles and to one relevant product page. You use anchor text that names the destination task so readers and search engines understand the connection. You avoid linking to login pages from public help articles because login links frustrate prospects who want answers first. Internal links build a help network that search engines can crawl and rank as a coherent section.

Connect Support Traffic to Trial and Demo Paths

You add a calm next step at the end of each help article. You invite readers to start a trial when the article covers a setup task and you invite them to book a demo when the article covers an advanced workflow. You keep the call to action to one sentence and you place it after the solution. Readers trust a next step more when it follows a complete answer.

You track help article traffic separately from blog traffic in analytics. You measure entrances and trial starts and demo bookings from help pages. You compare help pages by conversion rate and you improve the weakest high traffic pages first. A saas help center seo programme proves its value when you show trial starts from support content and not only page views.

You update help articles on the same day that a feature changes. You assign one owner to each help section and you review changed features in a weekly release note. You remove screenshots that show old screens because old screens confuse new users. Fresh help content keeps rankings stable after product updates.

You promote strong help articles through onboarding emails and in product tooltips. You link a new user to the exact help article that matches their first task. You reduce early churn when users complete their first task without friction. Help content then serves both search acquisition and customer success at the same time. SaaS onboarding content guide

Conclusion

You now hold a plan that turns support work into search growth. You map real questions and you structure clear help pages and you connect readers to trials. You update help content with every release and your help center keeps earning traffic.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should help center content stay public or gated?

Help center content should stay public for common tasks because public pages can rank and attract prospects. You gate only content that reveals account specific data or advanced admin settings for paying teams. Public help pages also reduce support load because users find answers without tickets. Gated help pages hide your expertise from search engines and from prospects.

How long should a help article be?

A help article should cover one task fully and stop when the task ends. Most task articles run between three hundred and eight hundred words depending on step count. You add screenshots only where a step needs visual guidance. You avoid padding because users want a solution fast and search engines reward direct answers.

Can help articles rank for competitor brand terms?

Help articles should target task questions and not competitor brand names. You answer questions about your product area in generic terms. You compare approaches only when you can stay factual and fair. Targeting a rival brand name in help content risks legal trouble and it attracts the wrong audience.

How do I choose which help articles to write first?

You choose articles for questions that appear most often in tickets and chats. You check search demand for each question and you prioritise high frequency questions with clear ranking gaps. You write setup articles before advanced articles because setup questions affect new users first. You revisit the list monthly as ticket trends shift.

Should I use video in help articles?

You can add a short video when a task involves many clicks across screens. You keep the video under three minutes and you add the same steps as text below the video. Text steps help search engines understand the page and they help users who prefer reading. Video alone leaves search engines with little text to rank.

How do I keep help content fresh after product updates?

You link each help article to the feature it documents in your release process. You review changed features each week and you update affected articles before release day. You assign owners by section so no article drifts without an owner. You archive articles for retired features and you redirect them to the replacement article.

Do help pages need internal links to blog posts?

Help pages benefit from links to blog posts only when the blog post adds useful context. You link a setup article to a use case post when the post shows a real workflow. You avoid stuffing help pages with promotional links because users came for a solution. Two relevant links per article keep the page focused and helpful.

How do I measure help center SEO success?

You measure search entrances and rankings for target questions and trial starts from help pages. You measure ticket deflection by comparing ticket volume before and after publication. You measure time on page and scroll depth for top articles. Strong help SEO shows growth in all four signals over a quarter.

Log File Analysis for Smarter Crawl Budget Decisions

Category: Technical SEO

Primary keyword: log file analysis

Meta description: Learn log file analysis with a clear routine for reading bot visits and fixing crawl waste so search engines focus on your key pages.

Log file analysis shows exactly which pages search bots visit and which pages they ignore. You read server logs and you count bot visits by page type and by status code. You use that evidence to fix crawl waste and to protect key pages.

Most site owners guess crawl problems from index reports alone. They assume search engines crawl every page equally and they miss bots that waste visits on filters and parameters. They fix the wrong pages and their key pages still wait for crawls.

You will finish with a clear log reading routine. You will spot wasted crawl and blocked pages and orphan pages fast.

Collect Server Logs Correctly Before You Analyse

You request raw access logs from your hosting provider or your server admin. You ask for at least thirty days of logs in a standard format with user agent and status code and URL. You store logs in a secure folder and you never share logs publicly because logs can reveal sensitive paths. Clean log access forms the base for every later decision.

You filter logs to search bot user agents and you separate each major bot into its own view. You keep Googlebot and Bingbot separate because each bot follows different crawl patterns. You remove visits from known monitoring tools and from your own office IP addresses. Clean filtering stops internal traffic from distorting bot counts.

You load filtered logs into a log analysis tool or a spreadsheet for larger sites. You group visits by URL pattern and by status code and by date. You calculate visits per page type so you can compare product pages against filter pages and tag pages. Grouping reveals patterns that a raw log file hides.

You check log completeness before you draw conclusions. You confirm that logs cover full days and that no days show zero bot visits from a server outage. You note any gaps in your report so you do not mistake missing data for low crawl activity. A log file analysis report stays honest only when it states its coverage period and its gaps. technical SEO audit guide

Read Bot Behaviour and Spot Crawl Waste

You rank URL patterns by bot visit count and you compare that ranking with your business priorities. You flag any filter or sort parameter that receives more bot visits than your product pages. You flag any internal search URL that bots crawl repeatedly because search result pages add little ranking value. Waste becomes visible when visit counts contradict page value.

You examine status codes for bot visits and you list every key page that returns an error. You separate server errors from not found errors because each error needs a different fix. You fix server errors first because they block both bots and visitors. A retailer reviewed logs and found bots visiting thousands of expired sale URLs daily while new product pages waited days for a first crawl. The team blocked expired parameters and new products gained crawls within days.

You study crawl frequency for new pages and updated pages. You measure days between publication and first bot visit for your last twenty articles. You investigate any new page that waits more than seven days when your site normally earns faster crawls. Slow discovery often points to weak internal links or a stale sitemap.

You look for orphan pages that bots never visit despite live status. You cross check your sitemap URLs against bot visited URLs and you list the gap. You add internal links to orphan pages from relevant hub pages and you confirm the next log cycle shows visits. Orphan pages cannot rank when bots never discover them through links.

Act on Log Evidence and Protect Key Pages

You block low value parameter URLs in your robots file only after log evidence proves waste. You test each block on a staging pattern and you confirm that key pages still receive bot visits. You avoid broad blocks that could hide faceted pages that actually rank. Evidence based blocks protect crawl budget without risking useful pages.

You update your sitemap to list only live and indexable pages that you want crawled. You remove redirected URLs and error URLs and noindex pages from the sitemap. You submit the clean sitemap and you watch the next log cycle for improved crawl focus. A log file analysis cycle closes only when you verify the change in fresh logs.

You strengthen internal links to pages that bots visit rarely but that carry business value. You add links from high traffic hub pages and from navigation where the page fits naturally. You avoid adding every page to the homepage because homepage links dilute focus. Targeted links guide bots toward pages that convert.

You repeat log review monthly and you compare bot visit patterns across months. You track visits to key page types and error rates and discovery speed for new pages. You document each action and its result in the next log cycle. Monthly repetition turns log data into a crawl management habit and not a one off investigation. crawl budget optimisation guide

Conclusion

You now hold a log based method for crawl decisions. You collect clean logs and you read bot behaviour honestly and you act on evidence. You repeat the cycle monthly and bots spend more time on pages that matter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where do I get server logs for my site?

Your hosting provider usually stores access logs in your hosting panel or on request. You ask support for raw logs in a standard format for the last thirty days. Cloud hosts often store logs in a logging service that you can export. You need server access or host support help when you manage a custom server.

How long should I keep log files?

You should keep at least ninety days of logs for trend comparison. You can archive older logs in compressed storage to save space. You delete logs that contain sensitive query data under your privacy policy. Ninety days gives you enough history to spot seasonal crawl changes.

Do I need a paid tool for log analysis?

You can analyse small sites with a spreadsheet and filtered log exports. Larger sites benefit from a dedicated log tool that groups URLs and bots automatically. You choose a tool based on site size and log volume. Small sites gain little from an expensive tool until log volume grows large.

What is a healthy crawl budget for a small site?

A small site with fewer than one thousand pages rarely faces a hard crawl budget limit. Search engines can crawl such a site fully when links and sitemaps stay clean. Crawl budget matters more for large sites with many parameter URLs. Small sites should still fix waste because waste delays discovery of new pages.

How do I identify Googlebot in logs?

You identify Googlebot by its user agent string and by reverse DNS verification. You check that the IP resolves to a Google domain before you trust the visit. Fake bots often claim the Googlebot name from other networks. Verification protects your analysis from spoofed bot traffic.

Can log analysis help with hacked pages?

Log analysis can reveal bot visits to unknown URLs that hackers created. You scan logs for strange URL patterns and for spikes in visits to new paths. You compare those paths against your sitemap and your content system. You remove hacked pages fast and you request a security review.

How often should I review logs?

You review logs monthly for routine crawl management. You review logs immediately after a migration or a major template change. You review logs weekly during a large site launch when discovery speed matters. Regular reviews catch crawl waste before it grows into an index problem.

What log metric matters most for SEO?

Bot visits to key page types matter most because they show whether search engines reach your value pages. Error rates for key pages matter next because errors block ranking. Discovery speed for new pages matters for fresh content sites. You track all three metrics together for a full picture.

Image SEO for Pages That Load Fast and Rank

Category: On-Page SEO

Primary keyword: image seo

Meta description: Learn image SEO with clear file naming and alt text and compression so your pages load fast and your images rank in search.

Image seo helps search engines understand your pictures and helps readers enjoy your pages. You optimise file names and alt text and compression before you publish. You place each image near the text that explains it.

Most site owners upload camera files with random names and huge file sizes. Search engines read no useful signal from a file named IMG 2049. Visitors on mobile wait for oversized images and many leave before the text loads.

You will finish with a simple image routine for every upload. You will name files clearly and you will write useful alt text and you will compress without losing quality.

Name and Size Every Image Before Upload

You rename each image with plain words that describe the visible subject. You keep the name short and you include the main subject and one context word. You avoid random numbers and camera codes because they teach search engines nothing about the image. Clear file names give search engines a first signal about image content.

You resize each image to the largest size it will display on the page. You never upload a four thousand pixel photo for a six hundred pixel display slot. You export the image in a web friendly format and you check the file size before upload. A travel blog resized hero images to display width and its mobile load time dropped sharply on article pages.

You compress images with a tool that preserves visual quality at smaller file sizes. You compare the original and the compressed version side by side at full display size. You accept compression only when text in the image stays readable and colours stay true. Compression protects speed and it keeps the image useful for readers.

You serve responsive sizes so phones receive smaller files than desktops. You set width and height attributes so the page layout stays stable while images load. You test the page on a real phone after upload because layout shifts hurt reader trust. Stable fast images improve the whole page experience. page speed optimisation guide

Write Alt Text That Serves Readers First

You write alt text that describes what a reader would miss if the image failed to load. You name the subject and the action and the context in one concise sentence. You write alt text for informative images and you leave decorative images empty so screen readers skip them. Reader first alt text also helps search engines understand image relevance.

You avoid stuffing alt text with keywords because keyword stuffed alt text reads poorly for screen reader users. You use the page topic naturally only when it matches the image content. You write different alt text for each image even when images look similar. A furniture store rewrote alt text for chair photos to name the material and the colour and the room setting. Image search traffic rose because each photo carried a distinct and accurate description.

You describe charts and diagrams with the key takeaway and not only the chart type. You state the trend or the comparison that the chart shows. You add the full data as text below the chart when readers need exact values. Accessible chart descriptions serve both search engines and users who cannot see the chart.

You review alt text during your regular content audit. You fix empty alt text on informative images and you fix alt text that repeats the caption word for word. You keep alt text concise and you remove any alt text that describes design elements. Clean alt text improves accessibility and it strengthens image relevance at the same time.

Place Images for Speed and Search Context

You place each image directly beside the paragraph that explains it. You avoid image galleries at the bottom of an article when the images illustrate specific steps. Context placement helps search engines connect the image with the surrounding text. Readers also follow steps more easily when the image sits next to the instruction.

You use a descriptive caption only when the caption adds information beyond the image itself. You name the location or the product detail or the step number in the caption. You avoid captions that repeat the alt text because repetition adds no value. An image seo routine treats captions as extra context and not as keyword space.

You lazy load images that sit below the first screen so the browser loads visible content first. You never lazy load the hero image because the hero image forms the first impression.
