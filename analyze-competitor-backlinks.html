How to Analyze Competitor Backlinks (And Turn Gaps Into Targets)

Competitor backlink analysis shows which sites link to rivals but not you. Here is the workflow: pick true competitors, extract gaps, qualify targets and build an outreach list.

Pick the right competitors

Your SEO competitors are the sites ranking for your target keywords — not always your business rivals. Pick three to five that consistently outrank you for your priority clusters; their link profiles show what your market rewards.

Extract the link gap

Using a backlink tool, list referring domains that link to two or more competitors but not to you. These sites have already demonstrated they link to companies like yours — the highest-probability outreach pool that exists.

Qualify before you outreach

A gap list is raw material, not a target list. Qualify each domain:

  • Relevance — does it cover your niche or your buyers' problems?
  • Quality — editorial standards, real audience, sane outbound patterns
  • Link type — editorial article, resource page, directory, partner page?
  • Replicability — what earned the link: content, data, a tool, a relationship?

Study their linkable assets

Look at which competitor pages attract the most links. Original research, free tools, templates and definitive guides dominate. If competitors earn links with an asset type you lack, that is a content investment signal, not just an outreach task.

Turn it into a campaign

Rank qualified targets by relevance and attainability, and work them in order — pitching guest posts, requesting insertions where appropriate and building the assets gaps reveal. How to read a competitor's anchor text

Anchor text shows how the web describes a competitor, and it often reveals what the competitor is known for. Export the anchor text of a competitor's backlinks and sort it by frequency. You will usually see three groups: brand names, plain URLs, and descriptive phrases that name a topic or a product. If a competitor's descriptive anchors cluster around one phrase, that phrase is probably a page they have invested in, and it tells you which topic they are winning. If their anchors are mostly branded, their links come largely from mentions, press and partnerships rather than from targeted content.

Finding resource pages and directories worth joining

Resource pages are lists of useful tools, guides and services on a topic, and they are one of the most replicable link sources a competitor analysis reveals. When a competitor appears on a resource page, open the page and ask whether your product or guide belongs there too. A good resource page is curated, updated, and clearly organised by topic. A poor one is a long list of links with no descriptions, added without review. The good ones are worth pitching, because the editor has already shown they link to companies like yours.

How to track competitors without copying them

Competitor tracking is useful until it becomes imitation. The point is to learn what the market rewards, not to reproduce a rival's site page by page. Set a simple routine: once a month, note which competitor pages gained new links, which new pages they published, and which topics they are covering that you are not. Then ask whether each move fits your own strategy. A rival's new integration page might matter to you if you serve the same buyers. A rival's new blog series on a topic outside your product probably does not, however many links it earns.

Turning one gap into a month of outreach

A link gap report can feel overwhelming, because it may list hundreds of domains. Turn it into work by choosing one gap and working it for a month. Pick a single competitor page that earns many links, understand what earned them, and build your own version that is genuinely better: clearer, more current, with a worked example or a useful template the original lacks. Then list the sites that link to the competitor's page, qualify them for relevance and quality, and work through them in order of fit.

For each site, tailor the pitch to what the site already does. A site that linked to the competitor's guide may welcome a better guide. A site that linked to the competitor's tool may welcome your tool. A site that mentioned the competitor in a roundup may welcome an updated entry. Send a small number of careful, specific pitches each week rather than a large batch of identical emails, and record the replies. At the end of the month, count the links earned, the replies received, and the lessons learned about which pitch worked. One gap, worked properly for a month, teaches you more about your market than a year of collecting gap reports you never act on.
