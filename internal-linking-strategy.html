How to Create an Effective Internal Linking Strategy

Internal links distribute authority, guide crawlers and help readers. Build a strategy with hubs, contextual links and anchor discipline — and audit it quarterly.

Why internal linking punches above its weight

Internal links are the one ranking lever fully in your control. They tell search engines which pages matter, distribute authority from strong pages to strategic ones, and help crawlers discover content. Most sites underuse them badly.

Step 1: Define your hub structure

Organize content into topic hubs: a pillar page per cluster, supported by related articles that all link back to the pillar and across to siblings where genuinely relevant. Your keyword map (clusters and target pages) is the blueprint.

Step 2: Link contextually, with intent

The highest-value internal links sit inside body copy, in sentences where the linked page genuinely helps the reader continue. Link from high-authority pages (homepage, popular guides) toward priority commercial pages — many sites discover their pricing page has almost no internal support.

Step 3: Use descriptive, varied anchors

Anchor text should describe the destination honestly. Vary phrasing naturally; repeating one exact-match anchor hundreds of times looks engineered and helps no one. Avoid 'click here' — it wastes the signal and hurts accessibility.

Step 4: Fix the failures

Audit quarterly for:

  • Orphan pages with zero internal links
  • Broken internal links and links to redirected URLs (update to final destinations)
  • Important pages buried more than 3–4 clicks deep
  • Over-linked boilerplate that dilutes contextual signals

Step 5: Make it a habit, not a project

Add internal-link review to your publishing checklist: every new article links to its pillar and 2–3 siblings, and gets linked from at least two existing pages at launch. Our on-page SEO service builds this into monthly campaigns so the structure stays healthy as you publish.

How many internal links should a page have?

There is no fixed number of internal links that suits every page, and chasing a number is the wrong approach. A short page that answers one question may need only three or four links: one to its pillar page, one or two to closely related articles, and one to a relevant commercial page where it fits naturally. A long guide that covers a broad topic may need ten or more, because it touches many subtopics a reader might want to explore. The test is not the count. It is whether each link helps a reader continue, and whether a reader who follows the links ends up on pages that matter to your business.

Linking from old pages to new pages

A new page starts with no internal links, and unless you link to it from existing pages, it may wait weeks to be discovered. Make this part of the publishing checklist. When a new article goes live, find two or three existing pages where it genuinely belongs, and add a link in the body copy, in a sentence that explains why the new page helps. A new guide on keyword research belongs in an older article about content planning, at the point where that article mentions choosing topics. A new comparison page belongs in an older buying guide, at the point where the guide discusses choosing between options.

Internal links and the buyer's journey

Internal links should follow the buyer's journey, not just the topic map. A reader who arrives on a top-of-funnel guide is still learning about the problem. The links on that page should lead to deeper guides and comparisons, not straight to a pricing page, because a reader who is not ready to buy will leave if pushed too soon. A reader on a comparison page is further along, so a link to the pricing page or to a free trial makes sense there. A reader on a pricing page needs links to the feature pages and to customer stories that answer their remaining doubts.

How to audit internal links with a crawl

A quarterly internal-link audit takes an afternoon and prevents slow decay. Run a crawl and export three lists: pages with no internal links, pages with broken internal links, and pages that receive very few links. Orphan pages are the first priority, because a page with no links is hard for crawlers to find and hard for readers to reach. Either link each orphan from a relevant page or remove it if it serves no purpose. Broken internal links are next, because they waste a reader's trust and a crawler's time. Update each one to its final destination, not to another redirect.

Then look at the pages that receive few links. If an important commercial page appears on this list, add links to it from your most visited guides, in sentences where it genuinely helps the reader. If a page receives many links but is no longer important, consider whether those links should point somewhere more useful. Finally, check for links that point to redirected URLs and update them to the final address, so crawlers and readers take one step instead of two. Save the crawl files and the list of fixes, and compare the next quarter's crawl with this one. A site whose internal links improve every quarter becomes easier to crawl, easier to navigate, and easier for readers to buy from.
