How Do You Write SEO Friendly URLs That Google and Readers Understand?

Write SEO friendly URLs that Google and readers understand. Keep slugs short, use hyphens and lowercase, and change old URLs only with a redirect.

Short answer: You learn how to write SEO friendly URLs for beginners by keeping the slug short, readable, and built from your main keyword. You use lowercase letters and hyphens, and you leave out dates, numbers, and extra words.

Most beginners copy the whole blog title into the URL, and the address turns into a long string nobody can read or share. They then lose clicks, when a short slug would tell the reader the topic at a glance.

You will write a clean slug for every new page, and you will fix old messy URLs with a redirect. You will then keep the structure simple.

A Good URL Tells the Topic at a Glance

A good URL tells the reader and Google what the page is about before anyone clicks it. You read the address and you know the topic, because the slug carries the main keyword in plain words. A reader who sees example.com/seo friendly urls knows the page teaches URL writing, while a reader who sees example.com/index.php?id=4821 knows nothing at all. You build the address from words, and you leave the numbers and codes out of it.

The Slug Is the Part You Control

The slug is the last part of the address, and it is the part you control on every page. You write it from the main keyword of the page, and you cut every word that adds no meaning. You keep it to three to five words, and you check that a stranger can read it aloud without stumbling. You set the slug before you publish, because changing it later needs a redirect and a little risk.

Short Slugs Beat Long Ones

A short slug beats a long one, because a short address is easier to read, share, and remember. You cut articles and filler words such as a, the, and, and of from the slug, and you keep only the words that name the topic. You compare a long slug with a short one side by side, and you pick the short one when it still names the topic clearly. You never sacrifice clarity for brevity, because a short slug that confuses the reader helps no one.

Build the Slug in Six Simple Steps

You learn how to write SEO friendly URLs for beginners fastest when you build each slug with the same six steps. You start with the page title, and you remove special characters, numbers, and superfluous words. You boil the title down to the keyword, and you add a modifier only when it helps the reader. You make the slug lowercase, and you replace the spaces with hyphens.

Start With the Title and Cut It Down

You start with the page title, and you remove every character that is not a letter or a number. You remove numbers that date the page, and you remove words that add no meaning. You keep the keyword and one or two words that sharpen it, and you drop the rest. You read the result aloud, and you cut one more word when the slug still feels long.

Use Hyphens and Lowercase Always

You separate words with hyphens, because Google reads a hyphen as a space and an underscore as a joiner. You write the whole slug in lowercase, because servers treat capital and lowercase letters as different addresses. You never use spaces, because a browser turns a space into an ugly code. You check the slug once before you publish, and you fix any capital letter at once.

Fix Messy URLs With Real Before and After Examples

You fix a messy URL by rewriting it into a short slug and adding a redirect from the old address to the new one. You never change a live URL without a redirect, because the old address may carry links and readers. You read each example below and note how the rewrite keeps the keyword and drops the noise. A small business in Karachi rewrote five messy URLs this way, and its pages kept their rankings because each old address redirected cleanly.

The new address reads example.com/email marketing guide, and it names the topic in three words. You cut the filler, and you keep the keyword that the reader searches for. You add the redirect, and you test the old address to confirm it lands on the new page.

Rewrite Two: The Numbered Product Page

The old address read example.com/index.php?id=4821, and it told the reader nothing about the product. The new address reads example.com/blue running shoes, and it names the product in three words. You replace the number with the product name, and the address becomes shareable in a message or a post. You add the redirect, and you update any internal link that still points to the old address.

You rewrite a dated slug the same way. The old address read example.com/2024/03/seo tips, and the date made the page look old within a year. The new address reads example.com/seo tips, and it stays fresh for years. You leave dates out of evergreen pages, and you update the content without the address looking out of date.

Keep the Whole Structure Simple

You keep the whole structure simple, because a clean structure helps Google crawl your site and helps readers find their way. You use subfolders to group related pages, and you keep the hierarchy shallow with one or two levels. You use HTTPS on every page, and you redirect the other version of your domain to the one you chose. Use Subfolders to Show the Hierarchy

You use subfolders such as blog or services to group pages by topic, and the folder tells the reader where they are on your site. You keep the hierarchy shallow, because a deep address hides the page from readers and crawlers. You use as many subfolders as you need, and you never add a folder that carries no meaning. You check the structure once a quarter, and you flatten any branch that grew too deep.

Change a URL Only With a Redirect

You change a URL only when you have a good reason, and you always add a 301 redirect from the old address to the new one. You update the internal links that pointed to the old address, and you check the sitemap for the new address. You test the old address after the change, and you confirm it lands on the new page. Frequently Asked Questions

What is an SEO friendly URL?

An SEO friendly URL is a web address that people and search engines can understand at a glance. It uses short readable words that describe the page, separated by hyphens, in lowercase, without session IDs or parameters. The key part is the slug, which should carry the main topic in a few words.

How long should a URL slug be?

A URL slug should run to three to five words, and the full address should stay under sixty characters when you can manage it. You cut articles and filler words, and you keep only the words that name the topic. You never sacrifice clarity for brevity, because a short slug that confuses the reader helps no one.

Should I use hyphens or underscores in URLs?

You use hyphens in URLs, because Google reads a hyphen as a space between words. You never use underscores, because Google reads an underscore as a joiner that glues the words together. You replace every space with a hyphen, and you check the slug before you publish it.

Should I include dates in my URLs?

You leave dates out of evergreen pages, because a date makes the page look old within a year. You keep the address clean, and you update the content without the address looking out of date. You use dates only when the page is tied to one event or one year.

What should I do when I change a URL?

You add a 301 redirect from the old address to the new one, and you update the internal links that pointed to the old address. You check the sitemap for the new address, and you test the old address to confirm it lands on the new page. You never change a live URL without a redirect.

Do keywords in URLs help rankings?

A keyword in the slug helps Google understand the topic of the page, and it helps readers trust the link before they click it. You place the main keyword in the slug, and you leave the rest of the address clean. You never repeat the keyword in the slug, because repetition adds nothing.

What is a URL slug?

A URL slug is the last part of the address, and it is the part that identifies a single page. You write it from the main keyword of the page, and you cut every word that adds no meaning. You set the slug before you publish, because changing it later needs a redirect.

You make it lowercase, and you replace the spaces with hyphens. You then keep the whole structure simple, and you change old URLs only with a redirect.
